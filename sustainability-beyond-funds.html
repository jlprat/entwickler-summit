Exhibit B

curl closes its bug bounty.

'19
Bounty opens on HackerOne. Over its life it pays out roughly $86,000 across 78 confirmed vulnerabilities.
mid-'25
Around 5% of submissions are genuine vulnerabilities. Roughly 20% are, in the maintainer's words, slop.
Jan '26
Twenty submissions in the first three weeks. Seven in one sixteen-hour window. Zero real vulnerabilities.
31 Jan '26
The bounty shuts down. curl is installed on something like twenty billion devices.

It gets better, and that matters: curl returned to HackerOne without cash rewards, and by April the valid-report rate was back above 15%. Removing the incentive worked. It also cost a year of someone's life.

Method

Which ones?

Semantically

Follow the graph down to what your product actually leans on, at any depth. Slower to work out, and it finds the load-bearing nodes nobody has heard of.

Hierarchically

Your direct dependencies only. Cheap to enumerate, and you'll miss the thing four levels down that everything rests on.

The question either method is answering: what stalls your roadmap if it stalls?

Method

The overlap rule

Pick the upstream work that is both something you need shipped and something the project actually wants.

That intersection is the whole strategy. Everything else is either a favour you'll stop doing or a fight you'll lose.

5492 / 017
The one genuinely actionable idea in the talk. Give it room. "If it's only in the left circle, you're building a fork. Only in the right, you're doing charity your CFO will cancel."
Method

When you get the overlap wrong

  • Only you wanted it. The patch doesn't land. You carry it downstream, and now you maintain a fork you never planned for — forever, quietly, with no owner.
  • Only they wanted it. It lands, you're thanked, and the work has no defender when budget season arrives. First thing cut.

The difference between a proving ground and an accident is whether you decided in advance when the fork gets deleted.

5492 / 018
This sets up Inkless in two slides' time — a fork built ON PURPOSE with a deletion date. Make the contrast explicit when you get there.
Second-order

Engineers who work in public, under their own names, on things that outlive the quarter, stay longer.

Their work has a permanent address that isn't your internal wiki. Their reputation compounds. And they can point at something in five years.

This is a real return, and it is not the reason to do it. If it's the only reason you have, the work doesn't survive a bad quarter.

THE ONE THAT GOT AWAY

KIP-1150

Diskless topics in Apache Kafka. Also: a case file with a different ending.

Shift of energy here. Four acts of decline, now one that worked. Don't oversell it as a happy ending — sell it as proof the death certificate was avoidable.
The pitch

It started as a business need, not a good deed.

  • Faster cluster upgrades.
  • Better recovery.
  • Lower infrastructure cost — less load, and much less cross-zone networking.

We looked for a way to tweak Kafka into using object storage natively. There wasn't one. It needed a rewrite of critical parts of the system.

Left circle of the Venn. Be blunt that it was self-interested — that's the point. Altruism doesn't survive budget season; a roadmap commitment does.
The screen test

Inkless — a fork built to be deleted.

October 2024. A fork of Apache Kafka, opened to answer one question: is diskless actually possible?

It was explicitly temporary from day one. The stated plan was always to delete it once the feature lands upstream.

5492 / 021
Explicit callback to slide 26. This is the payoff for the overlap rule and it's the most transferable lesson in Act VI.
Greenlight

2 March 2026

Apr 2025
KIP-1150 published. Deliberately written as a meta-KIP — it asks "do we want this?" and nothing else.
2025
The "how" splits into sub-proposals: KIP-1163 for the core produce and consume paths, KIP-1164 for coordination.
2 Mar 2026
Accepted. Nine binding votes, five non-binding.

Splitting the question was the strategic move. "Do we want to go this direction" is a conversation a community can have. "Approve my 40-page design" is not.

5492 / 022
The meta-KIP split is the reusable tactic — separate directional consensus from implementation review. Most people trying to land big upstream change get this wrong.

Kafka went to the cloud era through the front door.

Not through a proprietary fork, and not through a vendor's closed rewrite. A need we had became a direction the community chose.

No sponsorship cheque buys that. That's what co-producing means, and it's the whole argument for tier three.
